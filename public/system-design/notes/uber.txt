Understanding the Problem
- Match riders with available nearby drivers and coordinate the resulting trip lifecycle.
- Location updates are high-volume, approximate data; assigning a driver is a consistency-sensitive operation.

Functional Requirements
- Estimate a fare from pickup and destination.
- Request a ride and match it with a nearby available driver.
- Let drivers accept or reject offers and progress through pickup and drop-off.

Non-functional Requirements
- Keep matching latency low and driver locations reasonably fresh.
- Prevent a driver or rider from being committed to incompatible simultaneous trips.
- Retain ride requests and recover from connection loss, slow responses, and worker failures.

Core Entities
- Rider and Driver store identities and relevant profile/state information.
- DriverLocation records recent coordinates and update time.
- Ride stores pickup, destination, quoted fare, assigned driver, and lifecycle state.
- Offer or Reservation records a temporary driver claim, expiry, and attempt identifier.

API
- Request a fare estimate for pickup and destination.
- Create a ride using the quote and an idempotency key.
- Send driver location updates and accept or reject a specific offer token.
- Subscribe to ride status and retrieve the authoritative ride state after reconnecting.

High-Level Design


1) Estimate and request
- A pricing service estimates distance/time and returns a quote with an explicit validity period.
- Persist the ride request before acknowledging it and enqueue matching work reliably.
- Do not reserve a driver merely because a rider requests a quote.

2) Match and accept
- Query a geographic index for nearby available drivers and rank candidates using distance, travel time, and policy.
- Temporarily claim a candidate, send an offer, and await acceptance or expiration.
- Commit the driver and ride assignment atomically or through a carefully coordinated state machine.
- Delayed acceptance must include the current offer token so an expired offer cannot steal a newer assignment.

Deep Dives


1) Location ingestion and proximity
- Partition locations geographically and use geospatial cells or indexes to bound nearby-driver queries.
- Nearby searches must include adjacent cells when a rider is near a boundary.
- Store update timestamps and exclude stale driver locations from matching.
- Keep high-frequency location state separate from durable trip records so location writes do not overwhelm transactional storage.

2) Manage update volume
- Adjust reporting frequency to motion and activity; an idle driver need not send identical coordinates continuously.
- Batch or coalesce updates and discard superseded positions where only the latest location matters.
- Maintain a freshness budget; aggressive throttling must not make matching rely on obsolete positions.

3) Prevent double assignment
- Use conditional driver-state transitions or a transactional reservation so two matching workers cannot both claim the same driver.
- A distributed lock alone is insufficient if a stale worker can still commit after its lease expires.
- Include ownership/version checks when finalizing the assignment, and release claims safely on rejection or timeout.

4) Reliable matching and timeouts
- Durable queues prevent requests from being lost during peak demand or worker restarts.
- Retry matching with a bounded policy and a wider candidate search where appropriate.
- Persist offer deadlines and process expiry idempotently; recheck current state before releasing a driver.
- Client notifications are hints; reconnecting clients fetch the authoritative ride state.

Final Design
- A fast geospatial location service supplies candidates to durable matching workflows.
- Conditional reservations and offer tokens protect assignment consistency.
- Persistent ride state, retryable queues, and live status delivery connect estimation, matching, acceptance, and trip progress.
