Understanding the Problem
- Build a service that accepts immediate, future, and recurring jobs, then executes them reliably near their scheduled times.
- Scheduling decides when work becomes eligible; execution decides which worker runs it and how failures are retried.

Functional Requirements
- Submit jobs for immediate execution, a future time, or a recurring schedule.
- Execute due jobs and let users inspect their status.
- Retry failed attempts according to policy and retain execution history.

Non-functional Requirements
- Aim to begin jobs within about two seconds of their scheduled time.
- Support around 10,000 jobs per second in the scenario and tolerate scheduler or worker crashes.
- Provide at-least-once execution; side effects require idempotency because a crashed worker may have completed work before losing its acknowledgement.

Core Entities
- Job describes the task, payload reference, owner, and schedule.
- Execution identifies one scheduled occurrence with due time, status, attempt count, and result.
- Lease or claim state records which worker currently owns an attempt and when that ownership expires.
- Recurring definitions are separate from concrete executions so retries do not create extra scheduled occurrences.

API
- Submit a job with schedule and retry policy, returning a durable job ID.
- Query job and execution status, including failures and next scheduled time.
- Cancel or update pending work with version checks; define behavior if execution has already begun.
- Use an idempotency key when submitting to avoid creating duplicate jobs after a timeout.

High-Level Design


1) Schedule and dispatch
- Persist job definitions and due executions before acknowledging acceptance.
- A scheduler finds upcoming work using a due-time index and places eligible executions on a durable queue.
- Workers claim queued executions, run the task, persist the result, and acknowledge successful processing.
- Keep the payload small; large inputs live in storage and are referenced by the job.

2) Monitor status
- Store transitions such as scheduled, queued, running, succeeded, and failed with timestamps.
- Expose status without forcing clients to inspect the queue itself.
- Correlate logs and results by execution ID so repeated attempts remain distinguishable.

Deep Dives


1) Scheduling precision
- Repeatedly scanning the entire jobs table cannot satisfy tight timing at scale.
- Partition or index by scheduled time and poll a bounded upcoming interval.
- Use a short-term priority queue or timing structure for imminent jobs while retaining durable state for restart recovery.
- Handle clock skew and scheduler failover; persisted due times remain the authority after a process restarts.

2) Throughput
- Partition scheduled work and assign disjoint scheduler ownership to avoid duplicate dispatch storms.
- Horizontally scale workers independently from scheduling and separate queues for different resource classes.
- Apply backpressure and monitor oldest-job age; queue depth alone does not tell whether timing targets are being met.
- Recurring jobs create future execution records using stable occurrence IDs so retries do not schedule the same occurrence twice.

3) At-least-once execution
- A visibility timeout or lease allows abandoned jobs to become eligible again after a worker crash.
- Long-running workers renew leases; completion must be conditional on the current attempt or fencing token.
- Retry transient failures with backoff and move repeatedly failing executions to a dead-letter or inspection state.
- If an external side effect succeeded before a crash, replay may execute it again. Use downstream idempotency keys or transactional deduplication.
- Persist completion before acknowledging queue delivery, accepting that a lost acknowledgement can cause a safe duplicate attempt.

Final Design
- Durable schedule storage feeds partitioned schedulers and execution queues.
- Leased worker attempts provide recovery, while explicit status records support monitoring and cancellation.
- Stable occurrence IDs and idempotent task effects turn at-least-once delivery into predictable application behavior.
