Understanding the Problem
- Aggregate article metadata from many publishers into a fast, scrollable feed that links readers to the original publication.
- Separate publisher ingestion from serving the feed so slow or failing publishers do not delay user requests.

Functional Requirements
- Display an aggregated feed from thousands of publishers.
- Support continuous pagination and redirect readers to the publisher for full content.
- Consider categories and personalization as extensions to the baseline feed.

Non-functional Requirements
- Target feed reads below 200 ms and ingestion within 30 minutes of publication.
- Remain available during breaking-news traffic spikes and isolate individual publisher failures.
- Deduplicate repeated ingestion and preserve stable pagination as new articles arrive.

Core Entities
- Publisher stores source URLs, polling state, rate limits, and health.
- Article stores canonical URL, publisher, title, publication time, summary, and media references.
- FeedEntry stores ordering or ranking information; UserPreference and ReadingEvent support optional personalization.

API
- Fetch a feed with cursor, limit, and optional category or preferences.
- Retrieve article metadata and its publisher URL without proxying the entire publisher article.
- Ingestion workers submit normalized article records using canonical identifiers for deduplication.
- A cursor must include a stable tie-breaker and the ordering context used to construct the page.

High-Level Design

1) Ingest and serve articles
- A scheduler enqueues publisher-fetch jobs; workers read feeds or supported publication endpoints.
- Normalize timestamps, canonical URLs, and metadata, then deduplicate before storage.
- Build an indexed feed view and serve common pages from cache.
- Publisher fetch errors retry independently with backoff; one unavailable publisher must not block all ingestion.
- Clicking an article redirects to its original publisher, keeping the aggregator focused on discovery metadata.

Process: Ingest one publisher update
- Step 1: The source scheduler selects a publisher whose refresh is due and enqueues a fetch job with its source cursor or conditional-request metadata.
- Step 2: A worker fetches the source within its rate limits and parses article metadata and publication times.
- Step 3: It canonicalizes article identities and deduplicates records already seen from earlier fetches.
- Step 4: The worker commits new or changed articles and advances the source checkpoint only after required data is durable.
- Step 5: Derived feed indexes and caches receive the committed changes; optional thumbnail fetching or classification runs separately.
- Step 6: Failed fetches retry with backoff without holding up other publishers or marking an unsuccessful refresh as complete.

Deep Dives

1) Pagination consistency
- Offset pagination shifts when new articles arrive and becomes expensive at large offsets.
- Use a stable sort key plus article ID and a cursor identifying the last item.
- For mutable rankings, retain a snapshot or ranking-version context, or explicitly accept bounded feed movement.

Process: Serve the next news-feed page
- Step 1: The reader requests a feed with category/preferences, page size, and a cursor if continuing.
- Step 2: The feed service resolves the stable sort or ranking context and checks a compatible cached page or candidate set.
- Step 3: On a miss, it queries the indexed feed after the cursor, applies relevant filters, and selects a bounded result set.
- Step 4: It hydrates article summaries and returns them with the next stable ordering key and original publisher URLs.
- Step 5: Selecting an article navigates the reader to the publisher; subsequent feed requests continue from the returned cursor instead of a shifting offset.

2) Low-latency feeds
- Index the feed's ordering and filter fields and fetch only the columns required for a page.
- Cache shared top pages or precomputed feeds; hydrate article metadata in batches.
- Separate personalized cache keys from shared keys so users do not receive another user's feed.
- Invalidate or refresh derived views asynchronously with an explicit freshness target.

3) Publication freshness
- Poll publishers according to publication frequency and priority rather than using one interval for everyone.
- Use push or webhook mechanisms where available and polling as recovery.
- Track last successful fetch, discovered publication time, and queue age to detect stale sources.
- Rate-limit outbound fetches per publisher and reuse conditional requests to avoid downloading unchanged feeds.

4) Media and breaking news
- Serve thumbnails through an appropriate CDN or cached media path with bounded size and processing.
- Keep slow media fetching outside the feed request path and show usable metadata while media is unavailable.
- Breaking-news spikes require cache protection, request coalescing, and independent scaling of ingestion and serving.
- A popular article is a hot object even when article storage is well partitioned.

5) Categories and personalization
- Classify articles once during ingestion and maintain category-specific feed indexes.
- Capture reading events asynchronously and use them to produce candidate preferences or rankings.
- Blend cached shared candidates with user-specific ranking rather than building every page from the entire corpus.
- Preserve a useful generic feed for new users or unavailable personalization services.

Final Design
- Scheduled ingestion normalizes and deduplicates publisher data into durable article storage.
- Indexed or precomputed feeds, stable cursors, and caches provide fast reads.
- Independent media handling and measured source freshness keep both breaking-news traffic and publisher failures manageable.
