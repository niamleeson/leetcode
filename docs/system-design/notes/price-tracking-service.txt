Understanding the Problem
- The service records product prices over time. It alerts subscribers when a price meets their selected threshold.
- Collection, validation, history queries, and notifications have separate responsibilities and capacity needs.

Functional Requirements
- Users can view price history on the website or browser extension.
- Users can subscribe to a product with a price threshold and notification preference.
- The system discovers products and refreshes prices. Search, reviews, and comparisons across retailers are outside the initial scope.

Non-functional Requirements
- The scenario contains about 500 million products. The system cannot refresh every product at the same high frequency.
- History requests should complete within 500 ms. The notification target is within one hour of the qualifying price change.
- Favor availability, but distinguish unverified observations from trusted prices. Duplicate collection must not create misleading alerts.

Core Entities
- Product identifies a marketplace item, canonical URL, and collection state. User contains contact details and notification preferences.
- PriceObservation contains the product, price, currency, timestamp, source, and validation state.
- Subscription connects a user and product with a threshold and delivery settings.
- CrawlJob records scheduled collection. Notification records a qualifying price event and its delivery attempts.

API
- GET /products/{productId}/price accepts a period and granularity. It returns a bounded set of points for a chart.
- POST /subscriptions creates a threshold subscription. The Subscription Service also supports changes and removal.
- The extension reports product identity, observed price, and source evidence to the Price Update Service.
- A response distinguishes receipt from verification. Subscription status shows the active threshold and delivery settings.

High-Level Design

1) History and collection
- The initial Web Crawler collects product prices. The Price History Service queries stored observations by product and time.
- Normalize product identifiers, currency, and timestamps. Keep collection metadata in the Primary DB and history in the Price DB.
- Index history by product and time. Return a useful chart resolution instead of every stored observation.

Process: Read stored price history
- 1. The client requests a product and time range through the API Gateway.
- 2. The API Gateway routes the request to the Price History Service.
- 3. The service queries the Price DB for that product and range.
- 4. It returns ordered price points. The website or extension renders the chart.

2) Subscriptions and alerts
- The Subscription Service stores the user, product, and price threshold. An index supports product-based subscriber lookup.
- The initial Notification Cron checks price changes every two hours. This cannot meet the one-hour target in the worst case.
- The later design uses price-change events. Define whether an alert fires once, on each lower price, or after a threshold crossing.

Process: Check subscriptions with the initial cron job
- 1. The Subscription Service stores the user's product and threshold in the Primary DB.
- 2. Every two hours, the Notification Cron queries recent price changes.
- 3. For each change, it finds subscriptions whose thresholds match the new price.
- 4. It sends email notifications and records which notifications were sent. This interval can exceed the one-hour delivery target.

Deep Dives

1) Discover and refresh products
- A full catalog crawl spends too much work on rarely viewed products. A frontier queue records URLs awaiting collection.
- Prioritize products with active subscriptions, frequent views, or changing prices. Give less active products longer refresh intervals.
- The extension supplies observations for products that users already visit. It also discovers products missing from the catalog.
- Crawlers cover products without recent extension observations. Priority verification jobs check suspicious or conflicting reports.
- Keep source limits, retry delays, and crawl checkpoints. A failed request must not restart the catalog scan.
- Spread collection tasks across workers while preserving limits for each source. Priority alone does not remove source capacity constraints.

Process: Collect extension observations
- 1. The extension reads a product identifier and displayed price while the user views a product page.
- 2. It sends the observation to the Price Update Service.
- 3. The service normalizes the product and checks the observation. An unknown product creates a discovery record.
- 4. The service records accepted observations and their provenance. Suspicious reports enter the chosen verification policy.
- 5. The Scheduler gives verification jobs priority. Crawlers also refresh products without recent extension observations.

2) Validate malicious observations
- Validate format and plausible price ranges. Track report origin and reporter history. Several independent reports can increase confidence.
- The source compares consensus with immediate acceptance followed by verification. Immediate alerts can still spread an incorrect price before correction.
- For suspicious reports, a stricter option holds alerts until verification succeeds. This trades notification speed for fewer false alerts.
- Keep provenance and validation status. Correct bad observations without deleting the record of how the error occurred.

3) Notify efficiently
- Database change capture publishes price changes to the Price Change Stream. Workers query subscriptions whose thresholds match the new price.
- This replaces repeated scans for all changed prices. Split large recipient sets into bounded delivery work.
- Deduplicate each subscription and qualifying event. Recheck whether the subscription is active before sending.
- Retry temporary provider failures with delay. Retain a final failure state for inspection.
- Independent database and stream writes can diverge after failure. Use recoverable publication, such as change capture or an outbox.

Process: Send an event-driven price alert
- 1. A committed price change enters the Price Change Stream through database change capture.
- 2. A Price Change Worker reads the event. It queries matching subscriptions in the Primary DB.
- 3. The worker applies each subscription's threshold rule. It records a notification identity for the subscription and event.
- 4. Delivery work checks active preferences and sends the notification. It records the provider result.
- 5. Temporary failures retry with the same identity. A repeated price event must not create a new logical alert.

4) Fast historical charts
- Partition price history by product and time. A chart query should scan only the required range.
- Scheduled aggregation can store daily, weekly, and monthly summaries. This reduces reads but introduces aggregation delay.
- TimescaleDB is the source's alternative for partitioned time-series queries. Query performance still depends on indexes, data volume, and the requested range.
- Store popular ranges in a cache or use rollups when measurement shows a need. Return a resolution suitable for the chart width.
- Keep minimum and maximum prices when reducing detail. An average alone can hide a short price drop.

Process: Query a time-series price history
- 1. The client requests a product, time range, and aggregation interval.
- 2. The Price History Service queries the matching range in TimescaleDB.
- 3. The query groups observations into the requested intervals. The selected aggregates preserve the information needed for the chart.
- 4. The service returns ordered points and resolution information. The client renders the chart without fetching all raw observations.

Final Design
- The Crawler and Price Update Service collect prices. The Scheduler prioritizes refreshes and verification work.
- PostgreSQL stores products and subscriptions. TimescaleDB serves price history. Database change capture feeds the Price Change Stream.
- Price Change Workers find eligible subscriptions and send alerts. Provenance, deduplication, and explicit threshold rules protect notification quality.
