Understanding the Problem
- Design a chronological feed of posts from accounts a user follows.
- The main tradeoff is whether to assemble feeds when users read them or distribute post references when authors publish.

Functional Requirements
- Create posts and follow other accounts.
- View a feed containing posts from followed accounts.
- Page through older feed entries without repeatedly fetching the same results.
- Personalized ranking and media processing can be separate extensions.

Non-functional Requirements
- Feed reads must have low latency despite users following many accounts.
- Support a large user graph with heavily skewed follower counts and popular posts.
- Favor availability and bounded feed delay; posts and follow edges must remain durable.

Core Entities
- User and Post represent authors and durable content with creation timestamps.
- Follow edges support both listing a user's followees and listing an author's followers.
- FeedEntry stores a recipient, post ID, and ordering key; it is a derived view that can be rebuilt.
- A separate post cache lets many feeds reference the same content without copying it repeatedly.

API
- Create a post and return its ID after the durable write.
- Follow or unfollow an account using the authenticated user's identity.
- Fetch feed entries with a cursor and page size; batch-fetch the referenced post objects.
- Use stable ordering such as creation time plus post ID, rather than a mutable numeric offset.

High-Level Design

1) Posts and follows
- Route requests through stateless services to the post store and follow graph.
- Maintain indexes in both directions of the follow relationship because read-time and write-time fanout need different queries.
- Emit a post-created event after persistence; reliable publication avoids losing feed work when a process crashes after writing the post.

Process: Create a post and distribute feed entries
- Step 1: The author submits a post to the post service through the authenticated API.
- Step 2: The service commits the post and a recoverable publication record before acknowledging creation.
- Step 3: A publisher delivers the post-created event to fanout workers; duplicate delivery retains the same post identity.
- Step 4: Workers enumerate eligible followers and insert post IDs into their feed lists in bounded, idempotent batches.
- Step 5: High-follower-count authors can skip broad write fanout under the hybrid policy and be included during reads instead.
- Step 6: Failed batches retry from recorded progress; a retry does not create duplicate visible entries for the same recipient and post.

2) Assemble and paginate feeds
- A simple read-time feed fetches recent posts from followed users and merges the sorted results.
- Fetch enough candidates to fill the page after applying visibility and deletion checks.
- Return a cursor identifying the last ordering position. New posts can appear at the top without shifting previously read offsets.
- Keep authoritative posts separate from feed indexes so edits or deletions do not require rewriting every full feed entry.

Process: Assemble the next page
- Step 1: The reader sends a cursor and page size to the feed service.
- Step 2: The service reads the reader's precomputed entries and obtains recent posts from followed accounts assigned to read-time fanout.
- Step 3: It merges those candidate streams using timestamp plus a stable tie-breaker and discards duplicates or inaccessible posts.
- Step 4: It hydrates selected IDs through the shared post cache, reading the durable post store only for misses.
- Step 5: It returns the page and continuation key; the client stores that cursor for its next request.
- Step 6: Newly published posts appear ahead of the cursor rather than shifting an offset and causing the same old items to be returned again.

Deep Dives

1) Users following many accounts
- Precompute feed lists by distributing new post IDs to followers asynchronously.
- A read then fetches one recipient's ordered list, reducing scattered queries across many authors.
- Bound the number of stored entries and backfill older pages from durable data when necessary.
- Precomputation trades storage and write work for predictable read latency; it is most useful for active readers.

2) Users with many followers
- Synchronously writing millions of feed entries delays post creation and overloads the write path.
- Queue fanout work and process recipient batches with idempotent writes, retry limits, and lag monitoring.
- Use hybrid feeds: distribute ordinary posts on write, fetch celebrity posts on read, and merge both sets.
- Choose thresholds from fanout cost and traffic patterns, rather than assuming one policy fits every account.

3) Uneven post reads
- Hashing post IDs spreads different posts across cache nodes but cannot spread traffic to one extremely hot post.
- Replicate hot post values across multiple cache locations or serve them from local caches with bounded staleness.
- Distinguish cache partitioning from replication: more partitions alone do not fix a single hot key.
- Coalesce cache misses and protect the database during cache expiry or failure so viral traffic does not become a stampede.
- Invalidate deleted or restricted content promptly; cached feed IDs must not bypass access checks.

Final Design
- Durable post and follow stores feed asynchronous workers and derived feed lists.
- The read service merges precomputed ordinary posts with celebrity posts, then hydrates content from a shared cache.
- Stable cursors, bounded lists, idempotent fanout, and hot-object replication make the design scalable without treating the cache as the only copy.
