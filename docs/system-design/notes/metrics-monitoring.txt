Understanding the Problem
- Ingest service metrics, answer dashboard queries, and evaluate alert rules continuously.
- Time-series volume depends on both sample rate and label cardinality; many unique label combinations can dominate cost.

Functional Requirements
- Collect metrics from services and expose time-range queries for dashboards.
- Let users define threshold-based alert rules.
- Send notifications when alerts transition into actionable states.

Non-functional Requirements
- Keep dashboard queries responsive across weeks of data.
- Detect qualifying alerts in under a minute where required.
- Remain available during spikes and failures without allowing uncontrolled series growth.

Core Entities
- MetricSample includes metric name, timestamp, value, and labels.
- TimeSeries is a metric plus a unique label set.
- AlertRule defines query, threshold, duration, and recipient policy; AlertState tracks pending, firing, and resolved states.
- Retention and rollup metadata describe stored resolutions and lifetimes.

API and Data Flow
- Agents push batches or expose scrape endpoints; collectors normalize and validate incoming samples.
- Query APIs accept time range, series selectors, aggregation, and resolution.
- Rule APIs manage alert definitions independently from high-volume sample ingestion.
- Durable transport feeds time-series storage and alert evaluation, followed by notification delivery.

High-Level Design

1) Ingestion
- Pull-based collection centralizes scrape scheduling and target health; push handles environments that cannot be scraped directly.
- Batch samples, enforce limits, and buffer transient failures with bounded local or durable queues.
- Partition by series identity and time while watching for uneven tenants or hot series.

Process: Store a batch of metric samples
- 1. An agent gathers samples with timestamps and labels, or a collector scrapes the service's metrics endpoint.
- 2. The ingestion service authenticates the source and validates metric shape, tenant quotas, and cardinality limits.
- 3. Accepted samples enter bounded durable transport or the storage acknowledgement path required by the product's durability contract.
- 4. Consumers partition samples by series identity and time and write them to the time-series store, applying duplicate and late-sample rules.
- 5. They advance recoverable progress after required output is safe; rollup workers subsequently derive coarser historical resolutions.

2) Dashboards
- A time-series store indexes labels and organizes samples for efficient time-range scans.
- Fetch only the resolution needed for the chart rather than every raw sample over weeks.
- Cache repeated dashboard queries and avoid repeatedly scanning the same recent windows.

Process: Read a dashboard time range
- 1. The dashboard requests selectors, time range, aggregation, and display resolution.
- 2. The query service selects a compatible cache entry or the raw/rollup partitions appropriate to that resolution.
- 3. It narrows series through label indexes, scans only relevant time partitions, and combines aggregates with correct weights.
- 4. It returns bounded ordered points and freshness information for rendering rather than every raw sample in the interval.

3) Alerts and notifications
- Evaluate rules on a schedule or a streaming path with explicit handling of missing and delayed data.
- Require a condition to persist for a configured duration when useful to reduce transient false alarms.
- Persist alert state and notify on transitions, with grouping and cooldowns to avoid repeated notification storms.

Process: Evaluate a rule and notify on a transition
- 1. The evaluator loads an enabled rule and determines the time window whose data is sufficiently available for evaluation.
- 2. It queries or consumes the relevant samples and evaluates the threshold with explicit treatment of missing or delayed data.
- 3. It updates durable pending/firing/resolved state, including any required condition duration.
- 4. A qualifying state transition records a deduplicated notification intent; repeated evaluations of the same firing state do not automatically create new notifications.
- 5. Delivery workers group or route alerts and apply retries independently; a missing acknowledgement cannot erase the persisted alert state.

Deep Dives

1) Long-range queries
- Keep high-resolution recent data and downsample older samples into coarser rollups.
- Store enough aggregate information for the intended calculations; averaging averages without weights can be incorrect.
- Prune time partitions and use label indexes to limit the series scanned.
- Retention policies must align with dashboard and incident-investigation needs.

2) Low-latency alerts
- A separate alert evaluator avoids waiting behind expensive interactive dashboard queries.
- Streaming evaluation can reduce delay, but requires window state, late-data policy, and recoverable checkpoints.
- Track ingestion lag and evaluation lag separately; a fast evaluator cannot detect data that has not arrived.

3) Availability and cardinality
- Replicate critical ingestion and storage components and bound queues with explicit overload behavior.
- Avoid unbounded labels such as request IDs or arbitrary URLs; each unique combination creates another series.
- Enforce per-tenant series and sample quotas, detect sudden cardinality growth, and provide actionable rejection metrics.
- Dropping low-priority samples may be preferable to losing the entire pipeline, but the product must expose that degradation.

Final Design
- Collectors feed durable ingestion and partitioned time-series storage with tiered retention.
- Query services use rollups and caches; independent evaluators maintain durable alert state.
- Cardinality controls, lag monitoring, and notification deduplication keep the monitoring system useful during the incidents it must report.
